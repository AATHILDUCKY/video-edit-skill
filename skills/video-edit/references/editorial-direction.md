# Editorial decisions for engaging shorts

Treat the recording as a story, not a list of trigger words. Find one tension: repeated work, a mistaken assumption, a surprising result, or an unanswered question. Use the earliest credible phrase that states it. A strong first 1–2 seconds can be the speaker's own punchline followed by evidence; do not manufacture a sensational claim that the recording does not support.

Make an edit map before drawing. For each selected passage record: source interval, exact spoken phrase, editorial purpose, output interval, visual evidence, transition, and sound event. When the user specifies “at second 7 show X”, interpret that as output time unless they explicitly reference the source. If X conflicts with the spoken point there, briefly explain the adjustment and keep meaning intact.

## Active pacing for this creator

The creator wants frequent changes and fewer long text holds. For technical/personal-brand Reels, start with these editorial targets; adapt them to the actual speech and reading difficulty:

| Element | Starting direction |
| --- | --- |
| Opening | Put a truthful tension, useful result or personal decision in the first 1–2 seconds; show a relevant visual from the first spoken beat. |
| Visual state | Progress the idea roughly every 0.8–1.8 seconds: reveal evidence, move a request to the next stage, switch the relevant detail, or return to the speaker. |
| Shot/composition | Look for a meaningful cut or framing/composition change about every 1.5–3 seconds during energetic passages. Vary the intervals around clauses and reactions. |
| Headline | Use 2–6 words. Usually retire or replace it after about 0.8–1.6 seconds, once readable. Allow around 2 seconds or longer for essential names/complex reading. |
| Spoken captions | Default to at most 4 words and about 1.6 seconds per measured chunk; use 2–3 words for long Tamil tokens. Preserve every word and its measured timing. |

These targets express this creator's style; they are not a retention guarantee or a timer that authorizes cutting speech. A single long word or an unaligned segment may exceed the caption target. Correct alignment or let the speaker finish; never shorten measured speech to meet it.

Use a mix of clean cuts, a brief relevant insert, a restrained crop change and a diagram progressing through its actual stages. Within a longer explanation, change the evidence/detail while preserving a useful reference label. Do not leave a settled card and the same large headline sitting unchanged through several clauses. Recolouring a card, looping its entrance or adding a whoosh does not count as conveying a new beat. Choose the next visible state from the next spoken idea.

Keep a line of sight between attention and meaning: point → example/evidence → consequence → next point. Preserve required setup, the reason for the claim, caveats, negatives, technical names and the payoff. In particular, retain distinctions such as "currently exploring" versus "already built". Keep complete sentences or natural clauses at 1×. Remove redundant setup, false starts and expendable waiting, then inspect both sides of each cut. A natural reaction, emotional pause or readable demonstration can justify a longer hold; record that reason in the plan's editorial notes.

Before marking the edit complete, review any unchanged visual/title lasting more than roughly 2 seconds in an energetic passage. Add a useful beat, shorten the hold, or record why that hold helps comprehension. Check the muted picture for a clear progression and the audio for a complete argument. Compare each source point against the finished transcript and visuals: nothing necessary should disappear merely to raise the cut count.

Match density to content. A difficult technical concept needs an explanatory object and enough reading time; a familiar phrase can use fast kinetic type. Change visual state at meaningful words, demonstrations and musical accents. Avoid both static slides and constant meaningless stimulation. Use the speaker's expression as part of the edit; do not cover every second with an unrelated image.

Preserve a through-line object when useful: one task card becomes a process node; one packet travels through a network; one memory block changes capacity. Decide what it represents before animating. This creator explicitly requested their insta-follow-2.mp4 outro on every edit. Finish the thought first, then let the builder append it once. Additional logo metamorphoses or looping endings need an editorial reason.

Use transitions for relationships:

- Same object/idea: match its position, shape or direction across scenes.
- Cause → effect: let the action reveal the result.
- Comparison: hold reference scale and labels steady while the variable changes.
- New subject/time: a clean cut often reads better than a decorative wipe.
- Screen tutorial: cursor motion leads to the actual verified control, followed by the real result.

Trim false starts and redundant takes; retain human breaths and moments of comprehension. Small digital reframes can hide a jump cut when source resolution allows. Avoid obvious alternating zooms on every word. Keep the subject's eyes, hands or demonstrated screen region visible after crop. If portrait cropping destroys the subject, use fit/framed footage and meaningful surrounding graphics.

Captions transcribe speech; headline copy summarizes the idea. Do not make both repeat a full paragraph. Caption emphasis should identify meaning, not highlight every syllable. No invented percentage or fake “live result” as filler.

Sound has a hierarchy: speech first, meaningful action second, music third. Use a click for an actual click, keys only during visible typing, a subtle transient for a reveal. Repeated whooshes and impacts on every cut make a short tiring. End with a completed thought and enough time to understand the CTA; do not clip the final consonant to hit a round duration.
